## Why it matters

Every day you get messages on your phone and in your email, and some of them are scams that pretend to come from a bank, a delivery company or a government office, to take your data or your money. In this lesson you learn the signs that give these messages away, before you tap anything in them.

## The idea

A scammer never says they are a scammer. They pretend to be an organization or a person you trust, then try to make you act fast, before you think. This trick is called phishing, and it can reach you by text message (SMS), WhatsApp message or email.

Watch for these six signs:

1. **You were not expecting it.** It comes from a number you don't know, or it is about something you did not ask for, such as a prize in a contest you never entered.
2. **It claims to come from someone you trust:** a bank, a government office, a postal or delivery company, or someone you know.
3. **It rushes or scares you:** "within 24 hours", "your account will be suspended".
4. **It promises you something tempting:** a cash prize, or money waiting for you.
5. **It asks for your details or your money:** to update your details, send your card number, your PIN or a verification code, or pay a fee.
6. **It asks you to tap a link in it, or to call a number written in it.** A link is a word or an address you tap to open a page.

Do not relax just because a sign is missing: a scammer can fake the sender's number so that it looks familiar, and a message can come from the account of someone you know if their account was stolen.

You may see spelling mistakes in a scam message, but do not rely on them: some scams are well written and carry the name of an organization you know.

The next two lessons show why you never give anyone a verification code (the number you get to sign in to your account), and how to check a link before you open it.

## Step by step

We wrote these messages as examples of scam types that official bodies have warned about, including Saudi Arabia's Communications, Space and Technology Commission and its central bank. The amounts are made up.

### Example 1: a parcel waiting for payment (text message)

> **From:** a number you don't know
>
> Your parcel has arrived but we could not deliver it. Pay a 12-riyal delivery fee within 24 hours or the parcel will be returned. Pay here: (link)

**The signs:** a number you don't know, a short deadline, and a fee to pay through a link.

### Example 2: "Update your details" (text message)

> **From:** a number you don't know
>
> Dear customer, we have temporarily blocked your card. To reactivate it, update your details now using the link, or call customer service on the number below.

**The signs:** it claims to be your bank, scares you with a blocked card, and asks you to update your details through a link or a number in the message. The Saudi Central Bank has warned about messages that ask people to update their account details and send them to numbers and links that do not belong to the banks.

### Example 3: a prize in a contest you never entered (WhatsApp)

> **From:** a number you don't know
>
> Congratulations! Your number has won a prize worth 50,000 riyals. The offer ends today. To claim your prize, send your full name and your bank card number.

**The signs:** a prize in a contest you never entered, a short deadline, and a request for bank details.

### What to do if you are unsure about a message

1. Do not tap any link in it.
2. Do not reply to it, and do not call the number written in it.
3. Check for yourself, with a number you find yourself: the organization's number on its official website, or the number on your card if the message uses your bank's name. If the message claims to be from someone you know, call them yourself, by voice or video call, on the number you already had saved, not on the number the message came from.
4. Do not give your details to anyone until you have checked who they are. And never give your PIN or a verification code to anyone, even if they say they are from the bank.

## Try it

Open your text messages, WhatsApp and email, and look for a message that asks you to pay, update your details or tap a link. Check it against the six signs:

- How many signs did you find?
- If you found one or more, do not tap anything in it, and check it as in the steps above.

A sign does not always mean that a message is a scam, but it is reason enough to check for yourself. If you cannot find a message like this, apply the signs to the three examples.

## Key points

- A scam message pretends to come from an organization or a person you trust, and rushes you to act before you think.
- Its signs: it is unexpected, it scares you or promises a prize, it asks for your details or money, or it asks you to tap a link or call a number in it.
- If you are unsure: do not tap and do not reply, and check with the organization yourself, using its number or official website.
