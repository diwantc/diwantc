## Why it matters

You get a link in a message, tap it, and it opens a site that looks like your bank's or a government office's. But it is a fake site made by a scammer to take your password or card details. In this lesson you learn how to check a link before you open it.

## The idea

A link is the address of a page on the internet. Its most important part is the site name (domain): what comes after `https://` up to the first `/`. In the link `https://www.cst.gov.sa/ar/knowledge-center`, the site name is `www.cst.gov.sa`.

A scammer makes a site whose name is close to the real one: they change a letter, add a word, or change the end of the name. So:

1. **Read the site name letter by letter,** especially its end.
2. **Official Saudi government site names end in `.gov.sa`.** If a message says it is from a Saudi government body and the site name in its link does not end in `.gov.sa`, do not open it.
3. **`https` does not prove a site is real.** A link may start with `https`, which means the connection to the site is encrypted, that is, protected from eavesdropping. But it does not mean the site's owner is honest: government sites use it, and scammers use it too, to make their site look trustworthy.
4. **The safest choice is not to open a link from a message you did not expect.** Type the site name into the browser yourself, or open the organization's official app.

How can you tell that the message itself is a scam? See the lesson "Spotting scam messages".

## Step by step

### Read the site name in a link

1. `https://my.gov.sa`
   The site name is `my.gov.sa`. It ends in `.gov.sa`, so it is a government site.
2. `https://my.gov.sa.example.com`
   The site name is `my.gov.sa.example.com`. It starts with `my.gov.sa` but ends in `.example.com`, so it is not a government site.
3. `https://example.com/my.gov.sa`
   The site name is `example.com`. Here `my.gov.sa` comes after the first `/`, so it is not part of the site name.

We wrote the names that contain `example` only as examples.

### On iPhone: preview a link before opening it

This works for links inside pages in Safari.

1. Put your finger on the link and keep it there for a moment without lifting it (touch and hold).
2. A preview of the link appears without the page opening, with an Open button. Do not tap the preview or Open: both open the page.
3. Do not judge the site by how it looks in the preview: a fake site looks like the real one.
4. To close the preview and stay on your page, tap the screen away from it.

If your phone is Android, or you do not know how to see a link without opening it, do not open it. Type the site name yourself or open the official app.

## Try it

Open an old message that contains a link, without tapping the link. Read the site name in it, from after `https://` up to the first `/`: does it end the way you expect for the organization that sent it?

## Check yourself

**1. A message says it is from a Saudi government body. Which of these links could be its site?**

- a. `https://my.gov.sa.example.com`
- b. `https://my.gov.sa`
- c. `https://example.com/my.gov.sa`

b. In (a) the site name ends in `.example.com`, and in (c) the site name is `example.com`.

</details>

## Key points

- Read the site name in a link, from after `https://` up to the first `/`, letter by letter and especially its end.
- Saudi government site names end in `.gov.sa`, and `https` alone does not prove a site is real.
- Safest: do not open a link from a message you did not expect. Type the site name yourself or open the official app.
