## Why it matters

If you use the same password for your email and for a shopping app, and it leaks from the app, a scammer may try it on your email. Whoever gets into your email can change the passwords of your other accounts. In this lesson you learn how to make a strong password, and why each account needs a different one.

## The idea

A password is the secret key to your account. Scammers start by trying easy passwords, and a weak password can be found in seconds.

A weak password is one that is easy to guess, such as:

- Common passwords, like `123456` and `password`.
- Personal details: your name, a family member's name, a birthday, or your favourite team. Other people can find much of this on your social media accounts.
- Simple swaps, like typing the number `0` instead of the letter `o`. Scammers know this trick.

A strong password is long and hard to guess. An easy way to make one is a **passphrase**: several random words that have nothing to do with each other, written together. Its length makes it hard to find, and its words make it easy to remember.

There is a second rule that matters just as much: **a different password for every account**. If one password leaks from a website, your other accounts stay safe.

## Step by step

1. **Pick 4 or more random words that have nothing in common.** You can look around you and pick objects that are not related. Example: Lemon, Carpet, Orbit, Violin.
2. **Write them together, and start each word with a capital letter:** `LemonCarpetOrbitViolin`
3. **Count the characters.** It must be at least 16 characters long. Our example has 22.
4. **Add a number and a symbol.** The strength comes from the length and the random words, but Saudi Arabia's National Cybersecurity Authority (NCA) also recommends mixing capital and small letters, numbers and symbols. Example: `Lemon7Carpet#OrbitViolin`
5. **Use it for one account only.** Make a new passphrase for each account, the same way.
6. **Start with your email.** It is one of your most important accounts, because it is used to recover the passwords of your other accounts.

> Do not use the example on this page. Everyone who reads this lesson now knows it.

Never give your password to anyone, even if they say they are from your bank or from technical support. Trusted organizations do not ask for your password by message or phone call.

If it is hard to remember many passwords, a program called a password manager can create them and keep them for you. We will explain it in a later lesson.

## Try it

Make a passphrase now, for practice only, using the steps above. Type it in the notes app on your phone so you can count its characters, then check it:

- Does it have 4 or more words that have nothing in common?
- Is it at least 16 characters long? (Numbers and symbols count too.)
- Is it free of your name, your family's names and birthdays?
- Does it have a capital letter, a number and a symbol?
- Is it new, not used for any account?

If every answer is "yes", you have the method. Now delete the practice passphrase from your notes.

## Key points

- Make a passphrase: 4 or more random words, at least 16 characters, with a capital letter, a number and a symbol.
- Use a different password for each account, and start with your email.
- Never give your password to anyone. Trusted organizations do not ask for it by message or phone call.
