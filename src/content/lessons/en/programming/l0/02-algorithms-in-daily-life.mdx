## Why it matters

A guest who has never been to your home calls you, and you describe the way: "Go straight, then turn right at the mosque, then…" If you skip a step, or put one step before another, your guest gets lost. What you just described is an algorithm, and in this lesson you learn how to write one well.

## The idea

An algorithm is a plan for doing something: steps you write in order, to complete a task or solve a problem.

You follow algorithms every day, when you make a cup of tea, prepare a meal or tie your shoelaces.

The word has a story. "Algorithm" comes from the name of the mathematician Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi, who lived in the 9th century. His name was written in Latin as "algoritmi", and the English word came from it.

Before writing a program, a programmer often starts with the algorithm: they decide the steps and their order, then write them in a programming language, a language the computer understands.

A good algorithm has three things:

1. **A clear start and a clear end.**
2. **Precise, clear steps,** each with only one possible meaning.
3. **The right order.** If you move a step, the result may not be what you wanted.

Order matters even more with a computer. It carries out the steps as they were written. If a step is in the wrong place, the computer cannot tell by itself, and it keeps going.

## Step by step

### Write an algorithm for a cup of tea

Start with the task the way you would usually say it: "Make a cup of tea." A person understands this sentence, but it does not say what to do first, or when you are done.

Write it as ordered, precise steps:

1. Heat enough water to fill one cup until it boils.
2. Put one tea bag in the cup.
3. Pour the boiling water into the cup until it is full.
4. Wait 3 minutes.
5. Take the tea bag out of the cup.

Then check it:

- **Are the start and the end clear?** It starts with heating the water and ends when you take the bag out.
- **Is every step precise?** If you wrote "heat some water", the person following it would not know how much water to heat. If you wrote "wait a little", they would not know how long to wait. "Enough water to fill one cup" and "wait 3 minutes" are precise.
- **Is the order right?** If you moved step 3 before step 1, you would pour water that has not boiled yet.

### Order in a factory

In a drinks factory, computers control machines that fill bottles, then close them. If the program is written in the wrong order, so the machine closes a bottle before filling it, the computer will not know it is a mistake, and it will keep going.

Sometimes a program gives a result you did not want, because of a mistake in it. That mistake is called a bug. When it happens, go back to the algorithm you planned with: did you forget a step? Did you put a step in the wrong place?

## Try it

Pick a simple everyday task, such as setting an alarm on your phone. Write it in 4 to 6 ordered, precise steps.

Then give your steps to someone in your family, and ask them to follow them exactly as written, without adding anything of their own. If no one is with you, follow them yourself, word for word, as if you were a computer. Did the steps get the task done? If not, which step needs fixing?

## Check yourself

**1. Which of these is an algorithm?**

- A. "Make a cup of tea."
- B. "Heat enough water to fill a cup until it boils, then put a tea bag in the cup, then pour the water in, then wait 3 minutes, then take the bag out."
- C. "Tea is a hot drink."

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

B. It has ordered, precise steps, with a start and an end. A is a task with no steps, and C is a fact, not steps.

</details>

**2. "Wait a little, then take the bag out." What is the problem with these instructions, and how do you fix it?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

"A little" is not precise, and each person may understand it differently. Write an exact time, such as "wait 3 minutes".

</details>

**3. In the factory program, the closing step came before the filling step. Can the computer tell by itself that the order is wrong?**

<details>
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

No. The computer carries out what was written for it, and it cannot tell by itself that the order is wrong. That is why the programmer checks the order of the steps in the algorithm.

</details>

## Key points

- An algorithm is ordered steps to complete a task, and you follow algorithms every day.
- A good algorithm has a start and an end, precise and clear steps, and the right order.
- A computer carries out what was written for it and cannot tell by itself that the order is wrong, so the programmer plans with an algorithm and checks it.
